About the Museum

Who We Are

The Uganda Printing & Publishing Museum is a proposed heritage, learning and research space dedicated to preserving Uganda's printing and publishing story and making that history accessible to the public.

01

Who We Are

We bring together printing machinery, publishing equipment, official publications, historical documents, photographs, stories and oral histories to interpret how printed information helped shape Uganda.

Why the Museum Matters

Printing is more than machinery. It is a record of literacy, education, government, law, journalism, culture, political debate and national development. Preserving this heritage protects the physical evidence and human stories behind Uganda's information history.

02

Mission

To preserve, document, interpret and share Uganda's printing and publishing heritage through collections, exhibitions, education, digitisation and research.

Vision

A leading Ugandan centre for printing and publishing heritage, public learning, digital preservation and research — connecting the country's print past with its digital future.

The museum programme can work closely with UPPC, universities, libraries, publishers, printers, archives, government institutions and private collectors.
Our Purpose

Why Museum Matters

A national printing museum can preserve objects that are rapidly disappearing while giving students, researchers, professionals and the public a place to understand how information has been produced and shared.

Heritage

Preserve the Machines

Safeguard historic presses, typesetting systems, cameras, type, tools and finishing equipment before they are lost or dismantled.

Knowledge

Protect the Record

Document gazettes, books, newspapers, manuscripts, stationery, photographs and other printed evidence of national life.

Learning

Teach the Next Generation

Use demonstrations, workshops, exhibitions and digital resources to connect young people with print, media and information literacy.

Historical Journey

Government Printer → UPPC

A historical timeline of the Uganda Government Printer, later transformed into the Uganda Printing and Publishing Corporation (UPPC).

1902

Establishment of the Government Printer

Established in Entebbe under the British Protectorate. Its mandate was to produce the Uganda Gazette, laws, ordinances, and official notices.

Colonial Era Foundations
1920s–1950s

Administrative Expansion

The Government Printer expanded to handle administrative publications, annual reports, and colonial records.

Colonial Administration
1962

Independence & Transition

Uganda attained independence; the Government Printer continued as the official state printer, producing parliamentary acts, gazettes, and government communications.

Independence
1967

Republican Constitution

Under the new republican constitution, the Government Printer became central to disseminating decrees and laws.

Institutional Role
1970s

Official Publications During Political Upheaval

Despite political upheaval under Idi Amin, the Government Printer remained the sole authority for official publications, including decrees and propaganda.

Public Information
1992

UPPC Corporation

The UPPC Act formally transformed the Government Printer into the Uganda Printing and Publishing Corporation (UPPC), granting it corporate status while retaining its mandate as the official government printer.

Institutional Transformation
1990s–2000s

Commercial Expansion

UPPC expanded into commercial printing but faced competition from private presses, notably Nasser Road in Kampala.

Modernization & Challenges
2010s

Digital Workflows

UPPC began modernization efforts, including digital workflows, but struggled with funding and equipment upgrades.

Digital Transformation
2020s

Secure Printing & Partnerships

UPPC rebranded and emphasized its role in secure printing, including ballot papers, exams and permits, while exploring partnerships with educational and archival institutions.

Modern Era
Key Takeaway: The Government Printer, founded in 1902, evolved into UPPC in 1992. It remains Uganda’s official state printer, balancing its statutory role with modernization and competition from private presses.

Machines That Made the Page

Collection documentation on historical printing machines and equipment associated with UPPC. The supplied notes estimate the use of these machines at UPPC from the 1920s.

Manufactured 1912–1976

Intertype

Mostly used during the budgeting period because of its speed.

Commercial system from 1900

Monotype

Used mostly in printing books. Tolbert Lanston invented the system; the commercial hot-metal system combined a keyboard and casting machine.

Production continued into the late 20th century

Monotype Caster

Mostly used for finance bills containing many figures. The system used a perforated paper-tape keyboard and an automatic hot-metal caster.

Image transfer & processing

Camera

Majorly used for image transfer and processing as part of pre-press and reproduction workflows.

Manufactured from 1912

Ludlow Photograph Machine / Typograph

Designed to type big letters suitable for posters, letterheads, IDs and other display work. Commercial production began in the early 1910s.

Mid-1920s–1950s

Vicobold

Used for relief printing, embossing, debossing and foil stamping. The supplied notes associate the presses with Rockstroh-Werke AG and licensed UK manufacture.

Type & composition

Letter Blocks

Majorly divided into two types: single blocks and slugs, cast by the Monotype and Intertype machines respectively.

Why Preserve Them?

Benefits of Preserving Old Printing Machines

The machines are tangible evidence of Uganda’s publishing and printing heritage and can support education, tourism, research and institutional memory.

01

Historical Preservation

Safeguards Uganda’s publishing and printing heritage; provides tangible evidence of technological evolution; ensures future generations can see and understand the tools that shaped communication.

02

Educational Value

Offers hands-on learning for students, researchers and staff; demonstrates printing mechanics before digital technology; supports curriculum development in history, media studies and technical training.

03

Cultural Identity & Pride

Showcases Uganda’s journey in publishing and knowledge dissemination, strengthens national pride and acts as a cultural landmark for visitors and scholars.

04

Innovation Inspiration

Old machines highlight problem-solving and craftsmanship, spark creativity and encourage staff and visitors to think about how technology evolves.

05

Tourism & Public Engagement

Attracts local and international visitors and creates opportunities for guided tours, exhibitions and interactive displays while generating revenue and visibility through museum activities.

06

Institutional Legacy

Positions UPPC as custodian of Uganda’s publishing heritage, reinforces its role in knowledge preservation and dissemination, and builds credibility with donors and educational institutions.

07

Research & Documentation

Provides primary sources for academic research, enables comparisons between traditional and modern printing, and supports archiving projects and scholarly publications.

National History

Historical Timeline of Printing & Publishing in Uganda

A broader timeline connecting Uganda’s printing and publishing development with political, economic and technological transitions.

1894

Uganda Declared a British Protectorate

Printing was introduced mainly for administrative purposes.

Colonial Foundations
1903

Government Printer Established

The Government Printer was established and later formalized as UPPC, becoming the official printer for government gazettes, laws and notices.

Official Printing
1920s–1950s

Missionary Presses Expand

Protestant and Catholic presses produced religious texts, educational materials and early vernacular publications.

Missionary Publishing
1954

Industrial Capacity

Hydroelectric power at Owen Falls Dam boosted industrial capacity, including printing.

Industrial Growth
1962–1980s

Independence & Institutional Growth

UPPC continued as the official government printer while educational publishing, newspapers and political materials expanded.

Independence
1970s

Official Decrees & Propaganda

Idi Amin’s regime relied heavily on UPPC for official decrees and propaganda.

Public Information
1980s–2000s

Liberalization & Private Sector

Private printing firms grew, especially on Nasser Road in Kampala, which became Uganda’s printing hub.

Private Presses
1992

UPPC Act

The UPPC Act reaffirmed UPPC as the official government printer, responsible for sensitive documents such as ballot papers, exams and permits.

UPPC
1990s–Present

Modern & Digital Era

Offset and digital printing expanded; digital publishing, photocopy bureaus and small-scale presses diversified the industry. UPPC pursued modernization while private presses and digital publishing grew rapidly.

Modern Era

Present Day — Printing in Uganda is a Dual System

  • UPPC: Official government printer for gazettes and secure documents.
  • Private presses (Nasser Road): Dominant in commercial printing, books, flyers and branding.
  • Digital publishing: Growing rapidly, especially in education and media sectors.
Global Print Technology

Historical Timeline of Printing & Publishing Machines

From the first recorded impressions to digital and AI-connected printing.

3100 BCE–3000 BCE

Ancient Foundations

Cuneiform tablets in Sumer used wedge-shaped marks on clay; cylinder seals in Mesopotamia imprinted designs on clay.

868–1377 CE

Early Movable Type

The Diamond Sutra in China is identified as the earliest dated printed book using woodblock printing. Bi Sheng invented movable type using baked clay, and Jikji is the oldest surviving book printed with movable metal type.

1438–1455 CE

Gutenberg Revolution

Johannes Gutenberg developed an adjustable type mold in Mainz; the Gutenberg Bible became a major European movable-type publication and helped spark mass publishing.

1814–1875 CE

Industrial Advancements

Steam-powered and rotary presses transformed newspaper production and circulation, while offset printing was developed and later perfected.

1907–1970s

20th Century Innovations

Screen printing expanded applications; phototypesetting replaced hot metal typesetting; laser printing combined digital input with high-speed output.

1990s–Present

Digital & Modern Era

Digital presses enabled short-run and on-demand publishing; 3D printing expanded the concept beyond paper; AI-driven and cloud-connected presses now integrate automation, predictive maintenance and personalized publishing.

Key Takeaway: The printing press remains one of humanity’s most transformative inventions, enabling literacy, science and democracy. Digital and AI-powered presses continue that legacy through speed, customization and sustainability.

Museum Direction

Vision, Mission & Strategic Roadmap

A five-year direction for developing the UPPC Print Museum from a start-up heritage site into a flagship tourism attraction.

V

Vision

A premier national heritage center showcasing Uganda’s rich printing and publishing history, inspiring learning, innovation and cultural pride, and contributing to Uganda’s tourism growth.

M

Mission

To preserve, document and exhibit the history and evolution of printing and publishing in Uganda, providing an engaging cultural and educational experience that attracts local and international visitors, supports heritage tourism and promotes national identity.

Objective 1

Preserve Printing Heritage

Collect, restore and maintain historical printing and publishing artifacts, equipment and documents.

Objective 2

Enhance Visitor Experience

Develop interactive exhibits, guided tours and multimedia presentations to engage visitors.

Objective 3

Promote Cultural Tourism

Collaborate with Uganda Tourism Board and other stakeholders to position the Print Museum as a key heritage tourism destination.

Objective 4

Educate & Inspire

Offer educational programmes for schools, universities and vocational institutions to promote learning in printing technology and history.

Objective 5

Foster Partnerships

Partner with museums, cultural centres and printing industry associations locally and internationally for knowledge exchange and promotion.

Objective 6

Ensure Sustainability

Develop revenue streams through ticket sales, gift shop, publications, events and heritage-themed merchandise.

2025–2029

From Start-Up Heritage Site to Flagship Tourism Attraction

2025

Foundation & Launch

Renovate and set up the museum facility; acquire and catalogue at least 50 artifacts; develop branding, website and marketing; build tourism partnerships; launch a soft opening and school packages.

Milestones:
  • Facility completed and operational
  • At least 1,000 visitors
  • 3 formal tourism partnerships
2026

Engagement & Visibility

Expand interactive displays, host thematic exhibitions, train tour guides, introduce a small gift shop and launch marketing targeting schools, universities and domestic tourists.

Milestones:
  • 2,500+ annual visitors
  • ≥85% visitor satisfaction
  • 5 educational institutions with recurring visits
2027

Diversification & Regional Outreach

Partner with tour companies, launch an annual heritage printing festival, introduce multilingual exhibits, expand the collection to 150+ items and implement online virtual tours.

Milestones:
  • 4,000+ annual visitors
  • Featured in at least 3 national tourism campaigns
  • 3 regional tourism agency partnerships
2028

National Recognition & Tourism Integration

Work with UTB to enter national tourism itineraries, upgrade climate control, security and accessibility, host an international printing and publishing heritage conference and expand heritage learning and retail.

Milestones:
  • 6,000+ annual visitors, including 20% international tourists
  • Recognized as a heritage tourism site in Uganda
  • 300+ conference delegates from at least 5 countries
2029

Flagship Tourism Status

Achieve national and regional media coverage, introduce premium experiences, reach a self-sustaining revenue model and launch international touring exhibitions.

Milestones:
  • 8,000+ annual visitors
  • ≥90% visitor satisfaction
  • 25% international visitors
  • Financial self-sufficiency achieved

Strategic Enablers

Stakeholder collaboration with UTB, the Ministry of Tourism, Uganda Museums and private tourism operators; strong marketing and digital promotion; regular exhibition updates and innovation; and sustainable revenue streams and environmental best practices.
