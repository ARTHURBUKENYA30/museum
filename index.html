About the Museum

Who We Are

The Uganda Printing & Publishing Museum is a proposed heritage, learning and research space dedicated to preserving Uganda's printing and publishing story and making that history accessible to the public.

01

Who We Are

We bring together printing machinery, publishing equipment, official publications, historical documents, photographs, stories and oral histories to interpret how printed information helped shape Uganda.

Why the Museum Matters

Printing is more than machinery. It is a record of literacy, education, government, law, journalism, culture, political debate and national development. Preserving this heritage protects the physical evidence and human stories behind Uganda's information history.

02

Mission

To preserve, document, interpret and share Uganda's printing and publishing heritage through collections, exhibitions, education, digitisation and research.

Vision

A leading Ugandan centre for printing and publishing heritage, public learning, digital preservation and research — connecting the country's print past with its digital future.

The museum programme can work closely with UPPC, universities, libraries, publishers, printers, archives, government institutions and private collectors.
Our Purpose

Why Museum Matters

A national printing museum can preserve objects that are rapidly disappearing while giving students, researchers, professionals and the public a place to understand how information has been produced and shared.

Heritage

Preserve the Machines

Safeguard historic presses, typesetting systems, cameras, type, tools and finishing equipment before they are lost or dismantled.

Knowledge

Protect the Record

Document gazettes, books, newspapers, manuscripts, stationery, photographs and other printed evidence of national life.

Learning

Teach the Next Generation

Use demonstrations, workshops, exhibitions and digital resources to connect young people with print, media and information literacy.

Historical Journey

Government Printer → UPPC

A historical timeline of the Uganda Government Printer, later transformed into the Uganda Printing and Publishing Corporation (UPPC):

1902

Establishment of the Government Printer in Entebbe under the British Protectorate

Its mandate was to produce the Uganda Gazette, laws, ordinances, and official notices.

Colonial Era Foundations
1920s–1950s

Administrative Publications

The Government Printer expanded to handle administrative publications, annual reports, and colonial records.

Colonial Era Foundations
1962

Independence & Transition

Uganda attained independence; the Government Printer continued as the official state printer, producing parliamentary acts, gazettes, and government communications.

Independence & Transition
1967

New Republican Constitution

Under the new republican constitution, the Government Printer became central to disseminating decrees and laws.

Independence & Transition
1970s

Institutional Transformation

Despite political upheaval under Idi Amin, the Government Printer remained the sole authority for official publications, including decrees and propaganda.

Institutional Transformation
1992

UPPC Act

The UPPC Act formally transformed the Government Printer into the Uganda Printing and Publishing Corporation (UPPC), granting it corporate status while retaining its mandate as the official government printer.

Institutional Transformation
1990s–2000s

Commercial Printing

UPPC expanded into commercial printing but faced competition from private presses (notably Nasser Road in Kampala).

Modernization & Challenges
2010s

Modernization Efforts

UPPC began modernization efforts, including digital workflows, but struggled with funding and equipment upgrades.

Modernization & Challenges
2020s

Secure Printing & Partnerships

UPPC rebranded and emphasized its role in secure printing (ballot papers, exams, permits) while exploring partnerships with educational and archival institutions.

Historical Machines

Machines That Made the Page

about machines

1912–1976

INTERTYPE

was mostly used during the budgeting period because of its speed.

manufactured between 1912 and 1976

Commercial hot-metal system from 1900

MONOTYPE

Was used mostly in printing of books.

The first Monotype typesetting machine was invented by American engineer Tolbert Lanston, who patented his initial mechanical typesetting device in 1887. The commercial hot-metal system, featuring the keyboard and the casting machine, was brought to market in 1900

1900–late 1960s; last Caster manufactured in 1989

MONOTYPE-CASTER

Was mostly used during finance bill which had lots of figures.

Between 1900 and the late 1960s. Tolbert Lanston’s Monotype system—consisting of a perforated paper-tape Keyboard and an automatic hot-metal Caster—was first brought to commercial market in 1900. Production of these machines steadily continued until the last Caster was manufactured in Surrey, England, in 1989

Image transfer and processing

CAMERA

Was majorly used for image transfer and processing.

Manufactured starting in 1912

LUDLOW PHOTOGRAPH MACHINE

It was designed to type big letters suitable for posters, letterheads, IDs etc

The Ludlow Typograph was manufactured starting in 1912. First developed commercially in 1911, the hot metal typesetting machine was invented by William I. Ludlow and William A. Reade. Commercial production began in Chicago, and the machine found its way into printing plants around 1913 to 1914

Mid-1920s through the 1950s

VICOBOLD

It was used to for relief printing, embossing, debossing, and foil stamping.

Was manufactured from the mid-1920s through the 1950s. The presses were originally designed and built in Germany by Rockstroh-Werke AG around 1925, and were later manufactured under license in the UK by Frank F. Pershke

Estimated UPPC use from the 1920s

Letter blocks

LETTER BLOCKS: they are majorly into two types i.e single block and the Slag which are casted by the monotype machine and intertype machine respectively.

NB. The use of these machines at UPPC is estimated to be from 1920s

Why Preserve Them?

Benefits of Preserving Old Printing Machines

1

Historical Preservation

  • Safeguards Uganda’s publishing and printing heritage.
  • Provides tangible evidence of technological evolution.
  • Ensures future generations can see and understand the tools that shaped communication.
2

Educational Value

  • Offers hands-on learning for students, researchers, and staff.
  • Demonstrates the mechanics of printing before digital technology.
  • Supports curriculum development in history, media studies, and technical training.
3

Cultural Identity & Pride

  • Showcases Uganda’s journey in publishing and knowledge dissemination.
  • Strengthens national pride by connecting modern publishing to its roots.
  • Acts as a cultural landmark for visitors and scholars.
4

Innovation Inspiration

  • Old machines highlight problem-solving and craftsmanship of past eras.
  • Sparks creativity by showing how limitations led to breakthroughs.
  • Encourages staff and visitors to think about how technology evolves.
5

Tourism & Public Engagement

  • Attracts local and international visitors.
  • Creates opportunities for guided tours, exhibitions, and interactive displays.
  • Generates revenue and visibility for UPPC through museum activities.
6

Institutional Legacy

  • Positions UPPC as custodian of Uganda’s publishing heritage.
  • Reinforces UPPC’s role in knowledge preservation and dissemination.
  • Builds credibility with donors, partners, and educational institutions.
7

Research & Documentation

  • Provides primary sources for academic research.
  • Enables comparative studies between traditional and modern printing.
  • Supports archiving projects and scholarly publications.

Modern Era

Present Day – Printing in Uganda is a dual system:

  • UPPC: Official government printer for gazettes, and secure documents. New Vision is also a popular government owned printing and publishing company
  • Private presses (Nasser Road): Dominant in commercial printing, books, flyers, and branding.
  • Digital publishing: Growing rapidly, especially in education and media sectors.

Global Print Technology

Historical Timeline of Printing & Publishing Machines

Ancient Foundations

3100 BCE – Cuneiform tablets in Sumer

wedge-shaped marks on clay using stylus.

3000 BCE – Cylinder seals in Mesopotamia used to imprint designs on clay.

868 CE – Diamond Sutra in China: earliest dated printed book using woodblock printing.

Early Movable Type

1041 CE – Bi Sheng

Bi Sheng (China) invents movable type using baked clay.

1377 CE – Jikji (Korea): oldest surviving book printed with movable metal type.

Gutenberg Revolution

1438–1444 CE – Johannes Gutenberg

Johannes Gutenberg develops adjustable type mold in Mainz, Germany.

1455 CE – Gutenberg Bible: first major book printed in Europe with movable type, sparking mass publishing.

Industrial Advancements

1814–1875 CE

1814 CE – Steam-powered press by Friedrich Koenig: prints 1,100 sheets/hour, revolutionizing newspaper production.

1843 CE – Rotary press by Richard Hoe: continuous roll printing, enabling mass circulation.

1875 CE – Offset printing developed in England, later perfected in the U.S.

20th Century Innovations

1907–1970s CE

1907 CE – Screen printing patented, expanding into textiles and posters.

1950s CE – Phototypesetting replaces hot metal typesetting, improving speed and flexibility.

1970s CE – Laser printing introduced by Xerox, combining digital input with high-speed output.

Digital & Modern Era

1990s–Present Day

1990s CE – Digital printing presses (e.g., Indigo, Xerox DocuTech) allow short-run, on-demand publishing.

2000s CE – 3D printing emerges, expanding the concept of “printing” beyond paper to physical objects.

Present Day – AI-driven and cloud-connected presses integrate automation, predictive maintenance, and personalized publishing.

 Historical Timeline of Printing & Publishing in Uganda
Colonial Foundations (1894–1962)
1894 – Uganda declared a British Protectorate; printing introduced mainly for administrative purposes.
1903 – Establishment of the Government Printer, later formalized as the Uganda Printing and Publishing Corporation (UPPC). It became the official printer for government gazettes, laws, and notices.
1920s–1950s – Missionary presses (Protestant and Catholic) produced religious texts, educational materials, and early vernacular publications.
1954 – Hydroelectric power at Owen Falls Dam boosted industrial capacity, including printing.
Independence & Institutional Growth (1962–1980s)
1962 – Uganda gains independence; UPPC continues as the official government printer.
1960s–1970s – Expansion of educational publishing, newspapers, and political materials.
1970s – Idi Amin’s regime relied heavily on UPPC for official decrees and propaganda.
Liberalization & Private Sector Expansion (1980s–2000s)
1980s–1990s – Growth of private printing firms, especially on Nasser Road in Kampala, which became Uganda’s printing hub.
1992 – UPPC Act reaffirmed UPPC as the official government printer, responsible for sensitive documents (ballot papers, exams, permits).
1990s – Introduction of modern offset presses and limited digital printing.
Modern Era (2000s–Present)
2000s – Rise of digital publishing, photocopy bureaus, and small-scale presses serving education, NGOs, and SMEs.
2010s – UPPC struggles with funding and outdated equipment but begins rebranding and modernization efforts.
Present Day – Printing in Uganda is a dual system:
UPPC: Official government printer for gazettes, and secure documents. New Vision is also a popular government owned printing and publishing company
Private presses (Nasser Road): Dominant in commercial printing, books, flyers, and branding.
Digital publishing: Growing rapidly, especially in education and media sectors.

✅ Key Takeaway: Uganda’s printing and publishing history reflects its political and economic transitions — from colonial administration to independence, liberalization, and today’s mix of government and private sector printing.


Historical Timeline of Printing & Publishing Machines
Ancient Foundations
3100 BCE – Cuneiform tablets in Sumer: wedge-shaped marks on clay using stylus.
3000 BCE – Cylinder seals in Mesopotamia used to imprint designs on clay.
868 CE – Diamond Sutra in China: earliest dated printed book using woodblock printing.
Early Movable Type
1041 CE – Bi Sheng (China) invents movable type using baked clay.
1377 CE – Jikji (Korea): oldest surviving book printed with movable metal type.
Gutenberg Revolution
1438–1444 CE – Johannes Gutenberg develops adjustable type mold in Mainz, Germany.
1455 CE – Gutenberg Bible: first major book printed in Europe with movable type, sparking mass publishing.
Industrial Advancements
1814 CE – Steam-powered press by Friedrich Koenig: prints 1,100 sheets/hour, revolutionizing newspaper production.
1843 CE – Rotary press by Richard Hoe: continuous roll printing, enabling mass circulation.
1875 CE – Offset printing developed in England, later perfected in the U.S.
20th Century Innovations
1907 CE – Screen printing patented, expanding into textiles and posters.
1950s CE – Phototypesetting replaces hot metal typesetting, improving speed and flexibility.
1970s CE – Laser printing introduced by Xerox, combining digital input with high-speed output.
Digital & Modern Era
1990s CE – Digital printing presses (e.g., Indigo, Xerox DocuTech) allow short-run, on-demand publishing.
2000s CE – 3D printing emerges, expanding the concept of “printing” beyond paper to physical objects.
Present Day – AI-driven and cloud-connected presses integrate automation, predictive maintenance, and personalized publishing.

⚠️ Key Takeaway
The printing press remains one of humanity’s most transformative inventions, enabling literacy, science, and democracy. Today, digital and AI-powered presses continue that legacy, offering speed, customization, and sustainability.
