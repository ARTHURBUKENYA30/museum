OVERVIEW

About the Museum

The UPPC Print Heritage Museum is Uganda’s dedicated centre for the history of printing and publishing. It operates under the Uganda Printing and Publishing Corporation and serves researchers, students, craft practitioners and the general public.

Our galleries and research rooms interpret more than a century of print production — from early government notices and missionary tracts to modern newspaper and textbook manufacturing. Visitors encounter working knowledge, historic machinery and the documents that shaped civic life.

The museum combines conservation, exhibition and education. We preserve objects and records; we present them through programmes and displays; and we open selected holdings to scholars by appointment.

Located at the UPPC campus on Entebbe Road, Kampala, the museum is both a public destination and a working archive. Whether you are planning a first visit, arranging a school programme, or preparing a research request, this overview introduces the institution’s purpose, values and how to engage with our work.

Who we serve

The museum welcomes a wide range of audiences. School groups explore how print supported governance and learning. University students and independent researchers consult gazettes, newspapers and selected archival materials by appointment. Design and heritage professionals study type, machinery and production methods. Members of the public visit exhibitions that connect everyday documents to national history.

We work in partnership with UPPC staff, cultural institutions and educators to keep print heritage visible, accurate and useful — not only as nostalgia, but as a living record of how Uganda communicated with itself.

/ / History

History & Timeline

The story of formal government printing in Uganda spans more than a century. From early organised capacity to today’s digital workflows, print has underpinned law, education and public information. The timeline below highlights institutional milestones that the museum interprets through objects, documents and oral history.

1920s–1950s
Colonial Era Foundations

Establishment of the Government Printer in Entebbe under the British Protectorate. Its mandate was to produce the Uganda Gazette, laws, ordinances, and official notices.

1962s-1967
Independence & Transition

Uganda attained independence; the Government Printer continued as the official state printer, producing parliamentary acts, gazettes, and government communications.

1970s–1992
Institutional Transformation

Despite political upheaval under Idi Amin, the Government Printer remained the sole authority for official publications, including decrees and propaganda.

1990s–2000s
Modernization & Challenges

UPPC began modernization efforts, including digital workflows, but struggled with funding and equipment upgrades.

Today
Print Heritage Museum

A dedicated museum and research space opens the story of national print culture to the public, scholars and the next generation of craft and design practitioners.

How we deliver the mission

Our day-to-day work rests on three commitments: to preserve the material evidence of print; to interpret its meaning for diverse audiences; and to engage the public, scholars and practitioners in ongoing discovery.

Preserve

Conserve machinery, type, publications and oral histories that document Uganda’s print heritage, ensuring long-term care and responsible documentation.

Interpret

Present the social, political and technological story of printing through exhibitions, publications and programmes grounded in evidence.

Engage

Serve researchers, students, craft practitioners and the public with access, education and collaborative opportunities.

How we work

Collections care, research access and public programming are planned together. Objects are not only displayed; they are contextualised with documents and first-hand accounts. Education programmes draw on the same holdings used by researchers, so learning and scholarship reinforce each other.

We measure success by the quality of conservation, the clarity of interpretation, and the breadth of people who can meaningfully use the museum — from a school class on a first visit to a scholar completing a long-term project.

The museum is responsible for the long-term care of machinery, type, publications, photographs and oral history materials. Conservation work protects these holdings so they remain available for exhibition, education and research.

Preventive care

Stable storage, controlled handling and environmental monitoring reduce risk to paper, metal and photographic materials before damage occurs.

Treatment & stabilisation

When objects require intervention, treatments aim to stabilise condition for safe display or research use, documented for future reference.

Machinery & tools

Historic presses and workshop tools are cleaned, secured and interpreted as industrial heritage, with safety and authenticity both considered.

Documentation

Condition notes, photography and catalogue records support decisions about display, loans and research access over time.

Working with fragile print

Newsprint, ephemera and heavily used gazettes are especially vulnerable. Access may be supervised or limited so that material survives for future readers. Researchers are asked to follow handling guidance provided by staff.

Conservation priorities are reviewed alongside exhibition and education plans, so public programmes do not put collections at unnecessary risk.
