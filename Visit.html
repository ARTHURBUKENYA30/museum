Getting here

The museum is on the UPPC campus along Entebbe Road. Visitors should report to reception on arrival and ask for the Print Heritage Museum. Allow a few minutes for orientation before entering the galleries.

Public transport and private vehicles both serve Entebbe Road. Parking arrangements follow campus rules; ask at the gate if you need guidance on where to leave a vehicle.

What to expect

A typical general visit includes the exhibition galleries and selected interpretive displays of machinery, type and publications. Last entry is usually 30 minutes before closing so staff can secure the spaces safely.

Photography for personal, non-commercial use is usually allowed in gallery areas unless a label or staff member indicates otherwise. Research use of collections is arranged separately under Research Access.

Group Visits

Organised groups are welcome at the UPPC Print Heritage Museum. Booking in advance lets us prepare a guided introduction, allocate gallery space and match the visit to your aims — whether classroom learning, university teaching or professional interest.

Choose the category that best fits your group. Each section explains who it is for, what a typical visit includes, and practical notes on timing and group size.

Visit categories

Category A

Schools

  • Optional link to a classroom theme you name in advance

60–90 minutes · Book 2+ weeks ahead · State age range & class size

  • Staff briefing for lecturers on what students may handle

90–120 minutes typical · Academic contact required · Research Access for document use

Category C

Publishers, designers, heritage practitioners, government communications teams and partner institutions seeking a structured industry or collections visit.

  • Focus on machinery, type, official print or conservation practice
  • Conversation with staff on current exhibitions and collections priorities

  • Can be combined with a Support or partnership discussion if requested

By appointment · Weekday preferred · Outline your professional interest

Community & civic groups

Clubs, cultural associations, faith groups and civic organisations who want a general museum introduction without a formal curriculum brief.

  • Overview of the museum’s purpose and main galleries

What to expect on the day

Groups report to UPPC campus reception and are directed to the museum. A staff member or trained guide provides a short introduction, then leads or supports movement through the agreed gallery areas. Large groups may be split so everyone can see objects and labels clearly.

Photography for personal use is usually allowed unless a label or staff member indicates otherwise. Eating and drinking are not permitted in gallery spaces. Teachers and group leaders remain responsible for the behaviour and safety of their party throughout the visit.

How to book

Request a date at least two weeks in advance (longer in busy school terms). Email museum@uppc.co.ug and include:

  • Which category fits your group (A–D above)
  • Preferred date and time (and one alternative if possible)
  • Group size and age range (schools)
  • Institution or organisation name and a lead contact
  • Themes, exhibition interests or learning aims
  • Access, language or other support needs

We will confirm availability, outline the visit format and note any preparation useful before you arrive. For document or research-room use, also see .

Accessibility

We aim to make the museum as welcoming as possible. Because the site is part of a working UPPC campus, facilities can vary by building area. Contacting us before your visit helps us plan support and advise on the best route into the galleries.

Mobility

If you use a wheelchair or have limited mobility, please write ahead so we can advise on access routes, resting points and any steps or thresholds between campus reception and the museum spaces.

Sensory & other needs

Tell us about sensory preferences, communication support or other requirements. We will do our best to adjust timing, group size or interpretation within the limits of the site and collections care.

Assistance animals

Assistance animals are welcome where campus policy allows. Please mention this when you contact us so reception and gallery staff are prepared.

Quiet visits

If you prefer a quieter time, weekday mornings are often less busy. We can sometimes suggest quieter slots when you book in advance.

Contact us about access

Email museum@uppc.co.ug or call +256 414 251 267 with the date you hope to visit and a short description of the support you need. We will respond with practical advice for your visit.

Accessibility information is kept under review as campus facilities change. Your feedback helps us improve.

3D object studio

These models stand in for the kinds of machinery interpreted in our galleries. They are not scans of UPPC machines, but they help you understand scale and mechanism before you see the real objects on site.

Controls: click and drag to rotate · scroll or pinch to zoom · use fullscreen for a closer look.

Vandercook letterpress

Cylinder press used for proofing and short-run work

Look for the bed where the forme sits, the cylinder that carries the paper, and the overall height of the frame. Similar mechanical logic appears in production presses on the museum floor.

Common press

Early wooden press tradition (Franklin-type reconstruction)

Compare the upright posts and screw mechanism with later iron and steel machines. This form helps explain why early official printing was slow, skilled and specialised.

Guided gallery path

Four stops mirror the story you will encounter in the museum: how words were set, how they were printed, and how they reached the public.

Stop 1 of 4

The press room

This is where ink, pressure and paper met. Historic and industrial presses show how official forms, newspapers and textbooks were produced at scale — and why the print room was a centre of skilled labour.

What you will learn on site: how different press types changed speed and quality, and how UPPC’s production capacity supported national communication.
Stop 2 of 4

Type & composition

Before a page could be printed, every letter was selected and arranged. Type cases, composing sticks and matrices preserve the craft of setting the written word by hand.

What you will learn on site: how compositors worked, why accuracy mattered for law and education, and how digital pre-press later transformed this skill.
Stop 3 of 4

Official publications

Gazettes, notices and state stationery recorded law, administration and public information. These documents are not only archives — they are evidence of how government spoke to citizens on paper.

What you will learn on site: how official print underpinned governance, and how independence-era publications appear in the current exhibition programme.
Stop 4 of 4

Newspapers & public culture

Serial print and everyday notices shaped how people learned about national and local life. Newspapers and ephemera show print beyond the government office — in markets, schools and daily conversation.

What you will learn on site: how public print culture grew alongside official production, and why fragile newsprint needs careful conservation.

End of the online path

You have followed the core story of the museum: craft, production and public communication. A physical visit adds the presence of real machines, original documents and the campus setting on Entebbe Road.
